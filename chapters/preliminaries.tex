\section{Definitions}
\label{sec:Definitions}

This section fixes the notation and terminology used throughout the report. It
extends the notation of Section~\ref{sec:Problem Statement}: $\pi_\theta$ is the
student, $\mathcal{M} = \{M_1, \dots, M_K\}$ the providers and
$\mathcal{X}_1, \dots, \mathcal{X}_D$ the prompt domains.

\subsection{Providers and Access}

\begin{Definitions}[Provider]
A provider $M_k$ is a party that holds a language model and lets the student learn
from it. Each provider is characterised by its access mode and its unknown competence
on each domain. A provider is also called a teacher.
\end{Definitions}

A tokenizer maps a string to a sequence of tokens drawn from a finite vocabulary
$\mathcal{V}$. Models from different families generally use different tokenizers, so
their next-token distributions are not defined over a common support.

\begin{Definitions}[White-box and black-box access]
A provider is white-box accessible if, for any context $c$, its next-token
distribution $p_k(\cdot \mid c)$ over its vocabulary can be read. It is black-box
accessible if it can be queried only as a sampling oracle: given a text prompt $c$, it
returns generated text $y \sim M_k(\cdot \mid c)$, and neither its logits nor its
tokenizer are observable.
\end{Definitions}

The refined problem of this thesis assumes white-box providers that share the student's
tokenizer. The preliminary study of Chapter~\ref{chap:Evaluation} treated every
provider as black-box.

\subsection{On-Policy Distillation}

\begin{Definitions}[Off-policy and on-policy distillation]
In off-policy distillation the student is trained on responses sampled from a
teacher. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on provider responses is the standard instance,
minimising
\[
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\theta) =
  -\,\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{X}_d,\; y \sim M_k(\cdot \mid x)}
  \big[ \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x) \big].
\]
In on-policy distillation the responses are sampled from the student itself,
$y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$, and the teacher supplies supervision on the
sequences that the student actually produces.
\end{Definitions}

Off-policy training optimises the student only on the distribution of sequences that
the teacher produces. At inference time the student conditions on its own earlier
tokens, and a small deviation places it in contexts absent from its training data,
where further errors become more likely. This mismatch is termed exposure bias,
and the resulting growth of error with sequence length is termed compounding
error. On-policy distillation removes the mismatch by construction.

\begin{Definitions}[Divergences]
For distributions $p$ and $q$ over a common support, the Kullback--Leibler (KL)
divergence is
\[
  D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\|\, q) = \sum_{v} p(v) \log \frac{p(v)}{q(v)}.
\]
With the student as $p$ and a white-box teacher as $q$,
$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta \,\|\, p_{\mathcal{T}})$ is termed the reverse KL
and $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\mathcal{T}} \,\|\, \pi_\theta)$ the forward KL.
\end{Definitions}

Logit-based on-policy distillation minimises a per-token divergence of this kind along
student-generated sequences, and therefore requires both white-box access and a
shared vocabulary. Black-box on-policy distillation replaces the divergence with a
scalar reward derived from the provider's text, which is then optimised by policy
gradient.

\begin{Definitions}[Black-box on-policy distillation]
A black-box on-policy update samples $n$ student rollouts
$\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{n} \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$ for a prompt $x$, obtains $m$
reference responses $\{y^{(k)}_j\}_{j=1}^{m} \sim M_k(\cdot \mid x)$ from a provider,
assigns each rollout a reward
$r_i = R\big(x, y_i, \{y^{(k)}_j\}_{j=1}^{m}\big)$ that depends on the provider's
responses, and updates $\theta$ by a policy-gradient step on $\{(y_i, r_i)\}$.
\end{Definitions}

The reward function $R$ is a design choice. It may be a learned discriminator
\autocite{ye_gad_2025}, a rubric induced from the contrast between provider and
student responses and checked by a verifier \autocite{fang_ropd_2026}, or a
similarity between the student's continuation and provider continuations
\autocite{zhou_omniopd_2026}. The preliminary study of Chapter~\ref{chap:Evaluation} uses agreement with the
provider's final answers.

\subsection{Group Relative Policy Optimization}

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) \autocite{shao_deepseekmath_grpo_2024} is
the policy-gradient algorithm through which all on-policy rewards in this report are
applied.

\begin{Definitions}[Group-relative advantage]
For a prompt $x$, GRPO samples a group of $n$ rollouts $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{n}$ from the
current policy $\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}$ and obtains rewards $\{r_i\}_{i=1}^{n}$.
The advantage of rollout $i$ is
\[
  A_i = \frac{r_i - \operatorname{mean}\big(\{r_j\}_{j=1}^{n}\big)}
             {\operatorname{std}\big(\{r_j\}_{j=1}^{n}\big) + \epsilon},
\]
where $\epsilon$ is a small constant for numerical stability.
\end{Definitions}

The advantage measures how much better a rollout performed than the other rollouts
for the same prompt, which removes the need for a separately learned value function.
With the per-token importance ratio
\[
  \rho_{i,j}(\theta) =
  \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,j} \mid x, y_{i,<j})}
       {\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{i,j} \mid x, y_{i,<j})},
\]
GRPO maximises the clipped objective
\begin{align*}
  J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\Bigg[ \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{|y_i|}
  \sum_{j=1}^{|y_i|} \min\!\Big( \rho_{i,j}(\theta) A_i,\ 
  \operatorname{clip}\big(\rho_{i,j}(\theta), 1 - \epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}},
  1 + \epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}\big) A_i \Big) \Bigg]
  - \beta \, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\big),
\end{align*}
where $\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}$ bounds the size of each policy update,
$\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is a fixed reference policy, and $\beta$ controls the strength of
the KL penalty. If every rollout in a group receives the same reward, all advantages
are zero and the group contributes no gradient. A provider whose responses lead to
uniform rewards on a domain therefore teaches nothing there, however strong it is.

\subsection{Curricula and Selection}

\begin{Definitions}[Arm, round, selector and curriculum]
An arm is a provider--domain pair $a = (k, d)$. In each round $t$ the selector
chooses an arm $a_t$, possibly holding the same arm for a block of several rounds, and
the student is updated on a batch of prompts from $\mathcal{X}_{d_t}$ under the
supervision of $M_{k_t}$. A selector is a policy $\pi_{\mathrm{sel}}$ that maps the
history of contexts, arms and rewards to the next arm. The sequence of arms it produces
is the curriculum.
\end{Definitions}

\begin{Definitions}[Marginal value]
The marginal value of arm $(k, d)$ at student state $\theta$ is the expected gain in
utility from one round on that arm, $g(k, d; \theta)$, as defined in
Section~\ref{sec:Problem Statement}. An arm is dominant at $\theta$ if its marginal
value is at least that of every other arm.
\end{Definitions}

\begin{Definitions}[Fixed and adaptive curricula]
A fixed curriculum is decided before training, for example a single arm, a uniform
rotation over arms, or a set order of providers. An adaptive curriculum chooses each
arm using feedback observed during the same run.
\end{Definitions}

\begin{Definitions}[Learning curve]
The learning curve of a run is its utility as a function of the number of prompts the
student has trained on. Curricula are compared by their learning curves.
\end{Definitions}

The selector's feedback is distinct from the reward of the student's update. The former
scores a block of rounds for the selector, by the change in accuracy on the selection
set; the latter scores a single student response for the optimiser.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:Literature Review}

This section surveys the work on which provider-selective distillation builds.
Section~\ref{sec:lr-curriculum} covers curriculum learning, Section~\ref{sec:lr-opd}
on-policy distillation, Section~\ref{sec:lr-multiteacher} methods that distil several
teachers into one student, and Section~\ref{sec:lr-selection} the bandit tools a
curriculum over teachers can draw on. Section~\ref{sec:lr-gap} states the resulting
gap.

\subsection{Curriculum learning}
\label{sec:lr-curriculum}

Curriculum learning trains a model on examples ordered from easy to hard by a fixed
measure of difficulty, and was shown to speed convergence and improve the solutions
found \autocite{bengio_curriculum_2009}. Self-paced learning removes the external
measure by letting the model's own loss decide which examples are easy enough to
include \autocite{kumar_selfpaced_2010}.

Automated curriculum learning replaces the fixed order with a decision made during
training. A bandit chooses the next task and is rewarded by a measure of the learner's
progress on it, such as the change in loss or accuracy
\autocite{graves_curriculum_2017}. Teacher-student curriculum learning uses the slope of
the learning curve on each task as that reward, and also returns to tasks whose
performance is falling \autocite{matiisen_tscl_2017}. In reinforcement learning,
Prioritized Level Replay revisits the environment levels on which the agent can still
improve \autocite{jiang_plr_2021}. These methods share the principle that this thesis
adopts: practise where progress is being made.

The principle has been carried to language models. Online Data Mixing treats each data
domain as an arm of an adversarial bandit and adapts sampling weights from the training
loss on each domain \autocite{albalak_odm_2023}, using the EXP3 algorithm
\autocite{auer_exp3_2002}. DUET tunes a mixture across complete training runs by
Bayesian optimisation \autocite{chen_duet_2026}. The Self-Evolving Curriculum chooses
problem categories during reinforcement learning fine-tuning with a non-stationary
bandit whose reward is the size of the advantages a category produces
\autocite{chen_sec_2025}.

All of these choose what to practise, and their sources are fixed tasks or datasets.
None chooses whom to learn from. In distillation the teacher is a second axis of the
curriculum, and its usefulness depends on the student.

\subsection{On-policy distillation}
\label{sec:lr-opd}

On-policy distillation trains the student on its own samples, with a teacher scoring
each token of the sequences the student produces. Generalized Knowledge Distillation
(GKD) formulates this as minimising a divergence between student and teacher on
student-generated sequences \autocite{agarwal_gkd_2024}, and MiniLLM derives a
policy-gradient form of the reverse KL objective for the same purpose
\autocite{gu_minillm_2024}. Both descend from the imitation learning result that
training on the learner's own state distribution avoids the compounding errors of
behaviour cloning \autocite{ross_dagger_2011}. On-policy distillation has since been
presented as a post-training primitive that combines the on-policy relevance of
reinforcement learning with the dense supervision of distillation
\autocite{lu_thinkingmachines_opd_2025}, and its gains remain bounded by the quality
of the teacher \autocite{brown_opd_ceiling_2025}.

These methods require the teacher's next-token distribution over a vocabulary shared
with the student. Cross-tokenizer methods relax the shared-vocabulary requirement
while keeping logit access. ALM matches chunk-level likelihoods across tokenizers
\autocite{minixhofer_alm_2025}, the Universal Logit Distillation loss compares sorted
probability vectors under an optimal-transport distance \autocite{boizard_uld_2025},
and GOLD combines the two in an on-policy trainer \autocite{patino_gold_2025}. Any
fixed alignment between tokenizers nevertheless discards supervision where the two
segmentations disagree \autocite{sun_simct_2026,phan_tokenization_bias_2025}. The
motivating experiment of Section~\ref{pre:sec:motivating} measures this loss directly,
and is the reason the providers of the refined problem share the student's tokenizer.

Black-box on-policy distillation removes logit access altogether. Generative
Adversarial Distillation (GAD) trains a discriminator to distinguish teacher from
student responses and uses its score as a GRPO reward \autocite{ye_gad_2025}.
Rubric-based On-Policy Distillation (ROPD) induces a weighted rubric from the contrast
between teacher and student responses, scores each student rollout against it with a
verifier that is not told which model produced the response, and reports up to a
tenfold gain in sample efficiency over logit-based baselines across model families
\autocite{fang_ropd_2026}. OmniOPD supervises the student below the level of the full
response, by scoring high-entropy chunks of the student's reasoning against
continuations sampled from the teacher \autocite{zhou_omniopd_2026}. A study of on-policy distillation across federated clients shows that the student's
progress changes what it can next learn from its teacher, because the student generates
its own future training data \autocite{chen_fedtops_2026}; this is an instance of
performative prediction \autocite{perdomo_performative_2020} and the mechanism behind
hypothesis H2. Each of these methods assumes a single teacher that is consulted on every
training prompt.

\subsection{Multi-teacher on-policy distillation}
\label{sec:lr-multiteacher}

A single teacher caps the student at that teacher's competence and propagates its
errors. Multi-teacher on-policy distillation instead combines several teachers. MOPD
trains one specialist per domain with reinforcement learning, routes each training
prompt to the specialist for its domain, and distils on the student's own rollouts. It
recovers 91--95\% of each specialist's advantage over the student and has been used to
post-train an industrial model \autocite{ma_mopd_2026}. TrustMOPD removes the need for
domain labels by weighting teachers at every token, using each teacher's divergence
from a shared pre-reinforcement-learning reference as a proxy for its local
reliability \autocite{sun_trustmopd_2026}. MAD-OPD replaces a fixed teacher with a
debate among several teachers and weights each teacher's supervision by its
post-debate confidence, which lets a 4B student exceed its own 14B teacher on
LiveCodeBench \autocite{wang_madopd_2026}.

A stronger teacher is not always a better one. A teacher that is much larger than the
student can teach worse than a smaller one, which motivated inserting a mid-sized
teacher assistant between the two \autocite{mirzadeh_ta_2020}, and the same effect has
been measured for small language models learning from strong reasoners
\autocite{li_learnability_gap_2025}.

In the multi-teacher methods above, the routing or weighting rule is fixed by domain
labels or by a property of the teachers, and is not learned from how much the student
gains. An earlier line of work did learn whom to ask: RL-KD uses reinforcement
learning to assign weights to several teachers separately for each training instance
\autocite{yuan_rlkd_2021}. That selection was made for off-policy distillation of
task-specific models. Federated distillation likewise combines the predictions of
several parties, by averaging them on shared inputs
\autocite{li_fedmd_2019,lin_feddf_2020,fan_fedmkt_2025}, and every party is consulted
on every input.

\subsection{Bandits whose arms change the learner}
\label{sec:lr-selection}

Bandit theory supplies tools for several features of the problem. Discounted and
sliding-window upper-confidence-bound algorithms track arms whose reward distributions
change over time \autocite{garivier_ducb_2011}, and discounted Thompson sampling does
the same in a Bayesian form \autocite{raj_dts_2017}. Contextual bandits such as LinUCB
condition the choice of arm on an observed feature vector \autocite{li_linucb_2010},
which here can summarise the student's state. None of these models the case in which
the selector's own actions cause the change in rewards. Rotting bandits come closest:
the reward of an arm decays as that arm is pulled \autocite{levine_rotting_2017}. This
captures diminishing returns from a single source, but not the coupling present here,
where pulling any arm changes the student and with it the value of every other arm.

LLM routing learns to send each query to the cheapest model likely to answer it well
\autocite{ong_routellm_2025}. It measures a model's quality, not its fit to a learner,
and the evidence that small students learn less from strong reasoners than from simpler
ones suggests the two can differ \autocite{li_learnability_gap_2025}.

Two observations follow. First, curriculum methods choose what to practise from the
learner's progress, but their sources are tasks or datasets, not teachers. Second,
multi-teacher on-policy methods choose or weight teachers by domain labels or by
properties of the teachers, never by what the student gains. No existing method chooses
both the teacher and the domain from the student's own progress while training the
student on its own responses. This is the gap addressed by this thesis.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:Contributions}

This thesis makes the following contributions. The first three are complete; the last
two are in progress.

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt,topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Problem formulation.} It formalises provider-selective distillation as
    a curriculum over pairs of provider and domain, and identifies the marginal value
    $g(k, d; \theta)$ as the quantity a selector must estimate
    (Section~\ref{sec:Problem Statement}).
  \item \textbf{Testbed.} It provides a reproducible testbed with a small open student,
    providers of differing competence and three domains with verifiable answers
    (Chapter~\ref{chap:Implementation}).
  \item \textbf{A preliminary study with priced black-box providers.} It measures
    provider value by branching training from shared student checkpoints, and finds
    that providers of very different strength teach at a similar rate per gradient
    step, that a selector must learn from realised progress and not from headroom, and
    that learning from answer agreement alone is confined to one domain and unstable
    (Chapter~\ref{chap:Evaluation}).
  \item \textbf{A training framework for several white-box teachers.} It proposes an
    objective that combines a ground-truth reward with the selected provider's token
    probabilities, and a selector that scores each arm by its learned gain per round
    (Chapter~\ref{chap:Implementation}).
  \item \textbf{Evaluation by learning curves.} It compares adaptive curricula with
    fixed ones and tests whether a student trained on several providers passes the best
    single provider (Chapter~\ref{chap:Plan}).
\end{enumerate}
