\section{Definitions}
\label{sec:Definitions}

This section fixes the notation and terminology used throughout the report. It
extends the notation of Section~\ref{sec:Problem Statement}: $\pi_\theta$ is the
student, $\mathcal{M} = \{M_1, \dots, M_K\}$ the providers, $c_k$ the price of a
query to $M_k$, $\mathcal{X}_1, \dots, \mathcal{X}_D$ the prompt domains and $B$ the
query budget.

\subsection{Providers, Clients and Access}

\begin{Definitions}[Provider]
A provider $M_k$ is a party that holds a language model, and possibly private data on
which that model was trained, and that answers queries from the student. Each
provider is characterised by its access mode, its per-query price $c_k$ and its
unknown competence on each domain.
\end{Definitions}

The term provider is used in place of the federated learning term
client. In conventional federated learning a client holds data and trains a
copy of a shared model \autocite{mcmahan_fedavg_2017}; here a provider holds a model
and is never asked to train anything. The two settings share the constraint that the
data behind each party stays with that party.

A tokenizer maps a string to a sequence of tokens drawn from a finite vocabulary
$\mathcal{V}$. Models from different families generally use different tokenizers, so
the student vocabulary $\mathcal{V}_S$ and a provider vocabulary $\mathcal{V}_k$
differ and their next-token distributions are not defined over a common support.

\begin{Definitions}[White-box and black-box access]
A provider is white-box accessible if, for any context $c$, its next-token
distribution $p_k(\cdot \mid c)$ over $\mathcal{V}_k$ can be read. It is
black-box accessible if it can be queried only as a sampling oracle: given a
text prompt $c$, it returns generated text $y \sim M_k(\cdot \mid c)$, and neither its
logits, its vocabulary, nor its tokenizer are observable.
\end{Definitions}

All providers in this report are treated as black-box, including those whose weights
happen to be public. Under black-box access the student and a provider interact only
through text, and the cross-tokenizer problem does not arise.

\begin{Definitions}[Query and cost]
A query is a single prompt sent to one provider together with the response it
returns. A query to $M_k$ costs $c_k$, and a training run is budget-feasible if
the total cost of its queries does not exceed $B$. Queries issued to a fixed local
model that is not a provider, such as a verifier, are not charged against $B$ but are
reported separately.
\end{Definitions}

\subsection{On-Policy Distillation}

\begin{Definitions}[Off-policy and on-policy distillation]
In off-policy distillation the student is trained on responses sampled from a
teacher. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on provider responses is the standard instance,
minimising
\[
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\theta) =
  -\,\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{X}_d,\; y \sim M_k(\cdot \mid x)}
  \big[ \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x) \big].
\]
In on-policy distillation the responses are sampled from the student itself,
$y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$, and the teacher supplies supervision on the
sequences that the student actually produces.
\end{Definitions}

Off-policy training optimises the student only on the distribution of sequences that
the teacher produces. At inference time the student conditions on its own earlier
tokens, and a small deviation places it in contexts absent from its training data,
where further errors become more likely. This mismatch is termed exposure bias,
and the resulting growth of error with sequence length is termed compounding
error. On-policy distillation removes the mismatch by construction.

\begin{Definitions}[Divergences]
For distributions $p$ and $q$ over a common support, the Kullback--Leibler (KL)
divergence is
\[
  D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\|\, q) = \sum_{v} p(v) \log \frac{p(v)}{q(v)}.
\]
With the student as $p$ and a white-box teacher as $q$,
$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta \,\|\, p_{\mathcal{T}})$ is termed the reverse KL
and $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\mathcal{T}} \,\|\, \pi_\theta)$ the forward KL.
\end{Definitions}

Logit-based on-policy distillation minimises a per-token divergence of this kind along
student-generated sequences, and therefore requires both white-box access and a
shared vocabulary. Black-box on-policy distillation replaces the divergence with a
scalar reward derived from the provider's text, which is then optimised by policy
gradient.

\begin{Definitions}[Black-box on-policy distillation]
A black-box on-policy update samples $n$ student rollouts
$\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{n} \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$ for a prompt $x$, obtains $m$
reference responses $\{y^{(k)}_j\}_{j=1}^{m} \sim M_k(\cdot \mid x)$ from a provider,
assigns each rollout a reward
$r_i = R\big(x, y_i, \{y^{(k)}_j\}_{j=1}^{m}\big)$ that depends on the provider's
responses, and updates $\theta$ by a policy-gradient step on $\{(y_i, r_i)\}$.
\end{Definitions}

The reward function $R$ is a design choice. It may be a learned discriminator
\autocite{ye_gad_2025}, a rubric induced from the contrast between provider and
student responses and checked by a verifier \autocite{fang_ropd_2026}, or a
similarity between the student's continuation and provider continuations
\autocite{zhou_omniopd_2026}. The choice used in this report is specified in
Chapter~\ref{chap:Implementation}.

\subsection{Group Relative Policy Optimization}

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) \autocite{shao_deepseekmath_grpo_2024} is
the policy-gradient algorithm through which all on-policy rewards in this report are
applied.

\begin{Definitions}[Group-relative advantage]
For a prompt $x$, GRPO samples a group of $n$ rollouts $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{n}$ from the
current policy $\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}$ and obtains rewards $\{r_i\}_{i=1}^{n}$.
The advantage of rollout $i$ is
\[
  A_i = \frac{r_i - \operatorname{mean}\big(\{r_j\}_{j=1}^{n}\big)}
             {\operatorname{std}\big(\{r_j\}_{j=1}^{n}\big) + \epsilon},
\]
where $\epsilon$ is a small constant for numerical stability.
\end{Definitions}

The advantage measures how much better a rollout performed than the other rollouts
for the same prompt, which removes the need for a separately learned value function.
With the per-token importance ratio
\[
  \rho_{i,j}(\theta) =
  \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,j} \mid x, y_{i,<j})}
       {\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{i,j} \mid x, y_{i,<j})},
\]
GRPO maximises the clipped objective
\begin{multline*}
  J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\Bigg[ \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{|y_i|}
  \sum_{j=1}^{|y_i|} \min\!\Big( \rho_{i,j}(\theta) A_i,\ \\
  \operatorname{clip}\big(\rho_{i,j}(\theta), 1 - \epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}},
  1 + \epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}\big) A_i \Big) \Bigg]
  - \beta \, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\big),
\end{multline*}
where $\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}$ bounds the size of each policy update,
$\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is a fixed reference policy, and $\beta$ controls the strength of
the KL penalty. If every rollout in a group receives the same reward, all advantages
are zero and the group contributes no gradient. A provider whose responses lead to
uniform rewards on a domain therefore teaches nothing there, however strong it is.

\subsection{Selection}

\begin{Definitions}[Arm, round and selector]
An arm is a provider--domain pair $a = (k, d)$. In each round $t$ the
selector chooses an arm $a_t$, a batch of prompts from $\mathcal{X}_{d_t}$ is answered
by $M_{k_t}$, and the student is updated on the result. A selector is a policy
$\pi_{\mathrm{sel}}$ that maps the history of contexts, arms and rewards to the next
arm.
\end{Definitions}

\begin{Definitions}[Marginal value]
The marginal value of arm $(k, d)$ at student state $\theta$ is the expected gain in
utility per unit cost from one round on that arm,
$v(k, d; \theta)$, as defined in Section~\ref{sec:Problem Statement}. An arm is
dominant at $\theta$ if its marginal value is at least that of every other arm.
\end{Definitions}

\begin{Definitions}[Static and adaptive allocation]
A static allocation fixes, before training, the fraction of the budget spent
on each arm, and draws arms in proportion. An adaptive allocation chooses each
arm using rewards observed during the same run. The best static allocation in
hindsight is the static allocation that attains the highest final utility among a
fixed candidate set, chosen after all candidates have been run.
\end{Definitions}

\begin{Definitions}[Reward signal]
The selection reward $r_t$ is the scalar fed back to the selector after round
$t$. It is utility-based if it is the change in utility on the selection set,
$U_{\mathrm{sel}}(\theta_t) - U_{\mathrm{sel}}(\theta_{t-1})$, and a proxy
otherwise, for instance the reduction in training loss or a measure of disagreement
between student and provider.
\end{Definitions}

The selection reward is distinct from the on-policy reward $r_i$ of the black-box
update. The former scores a round for the selector; the latter scores a single
student rollout for the optimiser.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:Literature Review}

This section surveys the work on which provider-selective on-policy distillation
builds. Section~\ref{sec:lr-fl} covers federated learning and federated distillation,
and Section~\ref{sec:lr-fedllm} their extension to language model adaptation.
Section~\ref{sec:lr-opd} covers on-policy distillation and its black-box variants,
and Section~\ref{sec:lr-multiteacher} methods that distil several teachers into one
student. Section~\ref{sec:lr-fedopd} covers the first work to combine federated
collaboration with on-policy distillation. Section~\ref{sec:lr-selection} covers work
on choosing among sources under a budget, and Section~\ref{sec:lr-gap} states the
resulting gap.

\subsection{Federated learning and federated distillation}
\label{sec:lr-fl}

Federated learning trains a shared model across parties whose data cannot be pooled.
In Federated Averaging (FedAvg), each client runs several local gradient steps on its
own data and a server averages the resulting parameters
\autocite{mcmahan_fedavg_2017}. Averaging parameters requires every client to train
the same architecture and to disclose its weights, and the weights themselves can
leak information about the data on which they were trained.

Federated distillation relaxes both requirements by exchanging predictions in place
of parameters. In FedMD, clients with heterogeneous architectures share their class
scores on a public dataset, and each client distils the averaged scores into its own
model \autocite{li_fedmd_2019}. FedDF moves the distillation to the server, which
fuses an ensemble of client models into one by distilling their averaged outputs on
unlabelled data, and converges in fewer rounds than FedAvg
\autocite{lin_feddf_2020}. Selective-FD observes that an ensemble of client
predictions is only as useful as its members, and filters out ambiguous or
out-of-distribution predictions before they reach the student
\autocite{shao_selectivefd_2024}.

Two assumptions in this line of work matter for what follows. Every client is
consulted on every distillation input, so the only question is how to aggregate what
the clients return. Communication is the cost being minimised, while the cost of
asking any particular client is treated as uniform.

\subsection{Federated adaptation of language models}
\label{sec:lr-fedllm}

Federated learning has since been extended to every stage of language model
post-training. OpenFedLLM provides a framework for federated instruction tuning and
federated preference alignment, and reports that federated training on private
financial data lets a 7B model outperform GPT-4 on a financial benchmark, which
models trained on a single client's data do not \autocite{ye_openfedllm_2024}.
FedBiscuit carries reinforcement learning from human feedback into the federated
setting by training per-client binary selectors in place of a shared reward model,
and identifies preference heterogeneity across clients and reward hacking as the two
central difficulties \autocite{wu_fedbiscuit_2025}. With the shift to verifiable
rewards, GRPO has been federated as well. FedMOA lets each client reweight its
objectives during local GRPO training and aggregates models by task group
\autocite{wang_fedmoa_2026}. FedGRPO reverses the direction of transfer: a server-side
model sends candidate solutions to selected domain clients and aggregates only the
scalar rewards they return, so that neither data nor model updates leave the clients
\autocite{zhu_fedgrpo_2026}.

FedGRPO is the closest of these to the present setting in one respect: it decides
which parties to consult. For each question it retrieves similar auxiliary exemplars,
asks every client to report its competence on them, and sends the server model's
answer only to the most competent clients. Selection is therefore driven by each
client's competence on the question, not by how much the server model gains from
that client, and consulting a client carries no price beyond communication.

Other work transfers knowledge between language models of different sizes held by
different parties. FedMKT exchanges token-level predictions between a server-side
large model and client-side small models, aligning their vocabularies by minimum edit
distance \autocite{fan_fedmkt_2025}. FedCoT distils chain-of-thought rationales from a
server-side large model into client-side small models while perturbing the prompts
sent to the server, so that client data is not exposed in plain text
\autocite{fan_fedcot_2025}. DP-OPT makes the same concern formal for black-box
models: it tunes a discrete prompt on a local model under differential privacy and
transfers only that prompt to a cloud API \autocite{hong_dpopt_2024}. LaDA is the
federated method closest to the motivation of this thesis. A filter trained during
distillation predicts which reasoning samples are learnable for each client's small
model and allocates samples accordingly, and the study finds that a larger server
model does not necessarily yield a better client model \autocite{guo_lada_2026}.
LaDA adapts which samples a student receives as the student changes, but each
client learns from a single server model, so the question of which teacher to
consult does not arise.

Federated work on agents remains in-context rather than parametric. FICAL exchanges
textual knowledge compendia describing tool use, in place of model parameters, and
uses the aggregated compendium as a teacher for each client's agent
\autocite{wu_fical_2024}.

\subsection{On-policy distillation}
\label{sec:lr-opd}

On-policy distillation trains the student on its own samples, with a teacher scoring
each token of the sequences the student produces. Generalized Knowledge Distillation
(GKD) formulates this as minimising a divergence between student and teacher on
student-generated sequences \autocite{agarwal_gkd_2024}, and MiniLLM derives a
policy-gradient form of the reverse KL objective for the same purpose
\autocite{gu_minillm_2024}. Both descend from the imitation learning result that
training on the learner's own state distribution avoids the compounding errors of
behaviour cloning \autocite{ross_dagger_2011}. On-policy distillation has since been
presented as a post-training primitive that combines the on-policy relevance of
reinforcement learning with the dense supervision of distillation
\autocite{lu_thinkingmachines_opd_2025}, and its gains remain bounded by the quality
of the teacher \autocite{brown_opd_ceiling_2025}.

These methods require the teacher's next-token distribution over a vocabulary shared
with the student. Cross-tokenizer methods relax the shared-vocabulary requirement
while keeping logit access. ALM matches chunk-level likelihoods across tokenizers
\autocite{minixhofer_alm_2025}, the Universal Logit Distillation loss compares sorted
probability vectors under an optimal-transport distance \autocite{boizard_uld_2025},
and GOLD combines the two in an on-policy trainer \autocite{patino_gold_2025}. Any
fixed alignment between tokenizers nevertheless discards supervision where the two
segmentations disagree \autocite{sun_simct_2026,phan_tokenization_bias_2025}. The
motivating experiment in Chapter~\ref{chap:Evaluation} measures this loss directly.

Black-box on-policy distillation removes logit access altogether. Generative
Adversarial Distillation (GAD) trains a discriminator to distinguish teacher from
student responses and uses its score as a GRPO reward \autocite{ye_gad_2025}.
Rubric-based On-Policy Distillation (ROPD) induces a weighted rubric from the contrast
between teacher and student responses, scores each student rollout against it with a
verifier that is not told which model produced the response, and reports up to a
tenfold gain in sample efficiency over logit-based baselines across model families
\autocite{fang_ropd_2026}. OmniOPD supervises the student below the level of the full
response, by scoring high-entropy chunks of the student's reasoning against
continuations sampled from the teacher \autocite{zhou_omniopd_2026}. Each of these
methods assumes a single teacher that is queried on every training prompt.

\subsection{Multi-teacher on-policy distillation}
\label{sec:lr-multiteacher}

A single teacher caps the student at that teacher's competence and propagates its
errors. Multi-teacher on-policy distillation instead combines several teachers. MOPD
trains one reinforcement-learning specialist per domain, routes each training prompt
to the specialist for its domain, and distils on the student's own rollouts. It
recovers 91--95\% of each specialist's advantage over the student and has been used to
post-train an industrial model \autocite{ma_mopd_2026}. TrustMOPD removes the need for
domain labels by weighting teachers at every token, using each teacher's divergence
from a shared pre-reinforcement-learning reference as a proxy for its local
reliability \autocite{sun_trustmopd_2026}. MAD-OPD replaces a fixed teacher with a
debate among several teachers and weights each teacher's supervision by its
post-debate confidence, which lets a 4B student exceed its own 14B teacher on
LiveCodeBench \autocite{wang_madopd_2026}.

These methods share three assumptions that the present setting does not. Every
teacher is white-box and shares the student's vocabulary. Every teacher is available
on every prompt at no marginal cost. The routing or weighting rule is fixed by domain
labels or by a property of the teachers, and is not learned from how much the student
gains. An earlier line of work did learn whom to ask: RL-KD uses reinforcement
learning to assign weights to several teachers separately for each training instance
\autocite{yuan_rlkd_2021}. That selection was made for off-policy distillation of
task-specific models, with every teacher available at no cost.

\subsection{Federated on-policy distillation}
\label{sec:lr-fedopd}

The first work to combine on-policy distillation with federated collaboration studies
clients that hold disjoint prompt sets and share a single fixed teacher
\autocite{chen_fedtops_2026}. Each client runs on-policy distillation locally and a
server averages the resulting adapters. The study finds that FedAvg can perform no
better than independent local training at a small learning rate and clearly better at
a larger one. It attributes this to the student's dual role as learner and as
generator of its own future training data: averaging slows progress on a
prerequisite ability, which delays the student's arrival at states where the teacher's
supervision is useful, which in turn slows later learning. The proposed method,
FedTOPS, uses the teacher's agreement with the averaged model on cached rollouts to
choose how far to move along the averaged update, and improves macro accuracy over
FedAvg by 4.6 to 14.6 percentage points across six mathematical reasoning
benchmarks.

The mechanism this work identifies is an instance of performative prediction, in
which a model's deployment changes the distribution of data on which it is next
trained \autocite{perdomo_performative_2020}. It is also the mechanism behind
hypothesis H2 of this thesis. If what the student can learn from a teacher depends on
the states the student currently reaches, then which teacher is most useful should
also depend on those states. The FedTOPS setting does not test this, because it has
only one teacher, white-box access, and no query costs. Its federated parties hold
prompts rather than models.

\subsection{Choosing sources under a budget}
\label{sec:lr-selection}

Federated learning has its own selection problem, that of choosing which clients
participate in each round. Oort selects clients by an estimate of each client's
statistical utility and system speed \autocite{lai_oort_2021}, and biasing selection
toward clients with higher local loss has been shown to speed convergence
\autocite{cho_powerofchoice_2022}. In both cases the selected clients train the shared
model, and the utility estimate is a proxy computed from training loss.

Outside federated learning, the closest problem is data mixing. Online Data Mixing
treats each data domain as an arm of an adversarial bandit and adapts sampling
weights from the training loss on each domain \autocite{albalak_odm_2023}, using the
EXP3 algorithm \autocite{auer_exp3_2002}. DUET tunes a mixture across complete
training runs by Bayesian optimisation against noisy feedback from downstream tasks
\autocite{chen_duet_2026}. In both, the sources are fixed datasets and drawing from a
source has no price beyond the compute it consumes.

Bandit theory supplies the tools for the remaining features of the problem. Bandits
with knapsacks attach a resource consumption to each arm and stop when a budget is
exhausted \autocite{badanidiyuru_bwk_2013}. Discounted and sliding-window
upper-confidence-bound algorithms track arms whose reward distributions change over
time \autocite{garivier_ducb_2011}. Contextual bandits such as LinUCB condition the
choice of arm on an observed feature vector \autocite{li_linucb_2010}, which here can
summarise the student's state. None of these models the case in which the selector's
own actions cause the change in rewards.

Two further lines of work estimate the value of a source without training on it.
Active learning from multiple oracles learns each labeller's accuracy and queries the
most reliable \autocite{donmez_iethresh_2009}. LLM routing learns to send each query
to the cheapest model likely to answer it well \autocite{ong_routellm_2025}. Both
measure a source's quality, not its fit to a learner. The evidence that small
students learn less from strong reasoners than from simpler ones suggests these can
differ \autocite{li_learnability_gap_2025}. Once a run is complete, the contribution
of each party can be valued after the fact \autocite{sim_cml_2020}, which provides a
reference against which a selector's spending can be compared.

Three observations follow. First, federated methods that combine heterogeneous
language models either consult every party on every input or select parties by their
competence on the input, never by what the learner gains, and treat consultation as
free. Second, multi-teacher on-policy methods route or weight
teachers by domain labels or by properties of the teachers, never by what the student
gains, and assume white-box teachers that are free to query. Third, the one study of
federated on-policy distillation shows that the student's progress changes what it
can next learn from a teacher, but has a single teacher and so cannot ask which
teacher to consult.

No existing method chooses among several black-box providers, at a price, according
to the student's current state, while training the student on its own rollouts. This
is the gap addressed by this thesis.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:Contributions}

This thesis makes the following contributions.

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt,topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Problem formulation.} It formalises provider-selective on-policy
    distillation as a budgeted sequential decision problem over student states, and
    identifies the marginal value $v(k, d; \theta)$ as the quantity a selector must
    estimate (Section~\ref{sec:Problem Statement}).
  \item \textbf{Testbed.} It provides a reproducible testbed comprising a small open
    student, black-box providers that differ in domain competence, size and price,
    one deliberately degraded provider, cached provider responses, and verifiable
    tasks (Chapter~\ref{chap:Implementation}).
  \item \textbf{Empirical tests of non-dominance and state dependence.} It measures
    marginal value by branching training from shared student checkpoints, and tests
    whether the most valuable provider differs across domains (H1) and changes over
    training (H2) (Chapter~\ref{chap:Evaluation}).
  \item \textbf{Selectors and baselines.} It evaluates budget-aware adaptive
    selectors against uniform, strongest-only, cheapest-only and best-static
    allocations, with a greedy oracle as an upper reference, to test whether
    adaptivity pays (H3).
  \item \textbf{Reward signals and allocation analysis.} It compares utility-based
    selection rewards with the training-loss proxies used in data mixing and
    federated distillation, and compares each provider's share of the budget with a
    post hoc valuation of its contribution, including under a degraded provider.
\end{enumerate}
