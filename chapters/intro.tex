\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:Motivation}

Much of the knowledge a practitioner would like to train on is no longer available
as data. Hospitals, banks and firms bound by privacy regulation or commercial
interest rarely release their datasets, but they increasingly expose models trained
on them, either through an interface or as open weights. Alongside these sit
general-purpose and specialised models from many developers. For anyone training a
small language model, the resource at hand is therefore less often a dataset to
download and more often a collection of models to query.

Federated learning was introduced to learn from data that cannot be centralised
\autocite{mcmahan_fedavg_2017}. Its answer was to move parameters rather than data,
which requires every party to train the same architecture and to expose its
weights. Federated distillation relaxed the first requirement by exchanging
predictions on a shared public dataset in place of parameters
\autocite{li_fedmd_2019,lin_feddf_2020}, and recent work carries this exchange over
to language models of different sizes and tokenizers
\autocite{fan_fedmkt_2025,guo_lada_2026}. In each case every source is consulted on
every input, and the open question is only how to combine what the sources return.

Once sources are models behind an interface, that assumption becomes expensive.
Every query costs money, latency or a share of a provider's capacity, and providers
differ in domain, quality and price. The natural heuristic is to query the strongest
available model. The evidence suggests this is a poor rule for small students:
models of 3B parameters or fewer often fail to benefit from larger teachers and
learn more from simpler supervision \autocite{li_learnability_gap_2025}, and in
federated settings a larger teacher does not necessarily yield a better student
\autocite{guo_lada_2026}. Which provider is most useful thus depends on what the
student currently knows, and that changes as the student learns. A student with a
fixed tutoring budget does not book the most senior professor for every session; it
chooses tutors according to where it is weak and what it can absorb.

This thesis studies the resulting allocation problem. In the same way that work on
compute-optimal scaling asked how a fixed compute budget should be divided between
parameters and data \autocite{hoffmann_chinchilla_2022}, this thesis asks the
following question.

\begin{mdframed}[backgroundcolor=bg, linewidth=0pt, innertopmargin=8pt,
  innerbottommargin=8pt]
\itshape Given a fixed query budget and a set of black-box providers whose usefulness
is unknown, domain-dependent and changing, how should a student model decide whom
to ask, about what, and when?
\end{mdframed}

This setting is termed \emph{provider-selective distillation}. It differs from the
settings above in five respects.

\begin{description}[itemsep=2pt,topsep=4pt]
  \item[Black-box providers.] Each provider returns generated text only. Its weights,
    logits and tokenizer are not observable.
  \item[Private provider data.] A provider's training data never leaves the provider;
    only its responses to the student's queries do.
  \item[Heterogeneous competence.] No provider is assumed to be best in every domain,
    and the strongest model is not assumed to be the best teacher for the student.
  \item[Costly queries.] Each query carries a provider-specific price, and total
    spending is bounded.
  \item[State-dependent value.] The benefit of a provider's response depends on the
    student's current parameters, which the selector's own choices change.
\end{description}

The last property separates the problem from familiar tools. Online data mixing
treats sources as fixed datasets and adapts their sampling weights with an
adversarial bandit \autocite{albalak_odm_2023,auer_exp3_2002}. DUET tunes a mixture
across complete training runs using Bayesian optimisation
\autocite{chen_duet_2026}. Active learning from multiple oracles estimates the
reliability of each labeller but not its fit to the learner
\autocite{donmez_iethresh_2009}. LLM routing selects a model per query at inference
time, and no student is trained \autocite{ong_routellm_2025}. In
provider-selective distillation, by contrast, each decision changes the student and
with it the value of every provider at the next decision. Learning whom to ask and
learning from the answer are coupled, and neither can be studied in isolation.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:Problem Statement}

\paragraph{Setup.} Let $\pi_\theta$ denote a student policy with parameters
$\theta$, and let $\mathcal{M} = \{M_1, \dots, M_K\}$ denote $K$ providers. Each
provider is accessible only as a sampling oracle: given a prompt $x$, it returns a
response $y \sim M_k(\cdot \mid x)$ at a price $c_k > 0$ per query. Prompts are drawn
from a pool partitioned into $D$ domains,
$\mathcal{X} = \mathcal{X}_1 \cup \dots \cup \mathcal{X}_D$. The student is evaluated
by a utility
\[
  U(\theta) = \sum_{d=1}^{D} w_d \, \mathrm{Acc}_d(\theta),
\]
its weighted accuracy on held-out sets, one per domain, with weights $w_d \ge 0$
summing to one.

\paragraph{Interaction.} Training proceeds in rounds. At round $t$ the selector
observes a context $s_t = \phi(\theta_{t-1})$ summarising the student's state, such as
its per-domain accuracy on a selection set, and chooses an arm
$a_t = (k_t, d_t)$. A batch of $b$ prompts is drawn from $\mathcal{X}_{d_t}$ and sent
to provider $M_{k_t}$, and the student is updated on the returned pairs,
\[
  \theta_t = \operatorname{Update}\!\Big(\theta_{t-1},
  \big\{(x_i, y_i)\big\}_{i=1}^{b}\Big),
  \qquad x_i \sim \mathcal{X}_{d_t}, \quad y_i \sim M_{k_t}(\cdot \mid x_i),
\]
at a cost of $b\,c_{k_t}$. The selector then receives a reward $r_t$, either the
change in utility on the selection set or a cheaper proxy for it. Training stops at
the round $\tau$ after which no further query fits within the budget $B$.

\paragraph{Objective.} A selector is a policy $\pi_{\mathrm{sel}}$ that maps the
history of contexts, arms and rewards to the next arm. Its objective is
\[
  \max_{\pi_{\mathrm{sel}}} \; \mathbb{E}\big[\, U(\theta_\tau) \,\big]
  \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{t=1}^{\tau} b\, c_{k_t} \le B,
\]
where the expectation is over provider sampling, prompt sampling and the stochasticity
of training. The selection set that supplies rewards is disjoint from the test sets
on which $U(\theta_\tau)$ is finally reported.

\paragraph{Marginal value.} The quantity the selector must implicitly estimate is the
marginal value per unit cost of arm $(k, d)$ at student state $\theta$,
\[
  v(k, d; \theta) =
  \frac{\mathbb{E}\big[\, U\big(\operatorname{Update}(\theta, S_{k,d})\big) - U(\theta) \,\big]}
       {b\, c_k},
\]
where $S_{k,d}$ is a batch of $b$ prompts from $\mathcal{X}_d$ answered by $M_k$. A
selector that knew $v$ would play the arm of highest marginal value at every round.
In practice $v$ is unknown, is observed only through noisy rewards, and changes with
$\theta$, so each observation of an arm's value is partly stale by the time that arm
is next considered.

If $v$ did not depend on $\theta$, the problem would reduce to a stochastic
multi-armed bandit with costs. Because the selector's actions change $\theta$, rewards
are neither stationary nor independent of the selector's past choices. The problem is
more faithfully a sequential decision problem over student states, for which
contextual and non-stationary bandits serve as tractable approximations.

\paragraph{Hypotheses.} The formulation is worth studying only if provider value
actually varies in this way. Three hypotheses are therefore tested before any
selection method is proposed.

\begin{description}[itemsep=2pt,topsep=4pt]
  \item[H1 (Non-dominance).] At a fixed student state, the provider of highest
    marginal value differs across domains.
  \item[H2 (State dependence).] Within a fixed domain, the provider of highest
    marginal value changes over the course of training.
  \item[H3 (Adaptivity pays).] Where H2 holds, an adaptive selector reaches higher
    utility at equal budget than the best static allocation chosen in hindsight.
\end{description}

H1 alone justifies a static, per-domain allocation. H2 is what makes adaptive
selection necessary, and H3 is what makes it worthwhile. A negative result on H2 would
itself be informative, since it would show that a single allocation fixed before
training is sufficient.

\paragraph{Assumptions.} The following assumptions hold throughout this thesis.
There is a single student. Providers are honest, though they may be noisy or of low
quality; they do not act strategically. Provider responses to the prompt pool may be
generated once and cached, so that querying is simulated without repeated inference.
The student is updated by supervised fine-tuning on returned responses, with a
preference-based update considered as a variant.

\section{Objectives and Scope}
\label{sec:Objectives}

The objectives of this project are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt,topsep=4pt]
  \item Formalise provider-selective distillation as stated above, and construct a
    reproducible testbed comprising a small open student, providers that differ in
    domain, size and price, and one deliberately degraded provider.
  \item Measure marginal value empirically, by training from shared student
    checkpoints on each arm, in order to test H1 and H2.
  \item Design selection policies and evaluate them against uniform allocation,
    strongest-provider-only, cheapest-provider-only and the best static allocation
    in hindsight, with a greedy oracle as an upper reference.
  \item Compare reward signals, contrasting utility gain on a selection set with the
    training-loss proxies used in prior data-mixing and federated distillation work.
  \item Analyse the learned allocations, comparing each provider's share of the
    budget with a post hoc valuation of its contribution \autocite{sim_cml_2020} and
    examining robustness to the degraded provider.
\end{enumerate}

Four directions lie outside the scope of this project. The first is strategic
behaviour by providers, which turns the problem into one of mechanism design and
incentive-aware reward \autocite{sim_cml_2020}; it is discussed in
Chapter~\ref{chap:Conclusion}. The second is mutual transfer, in which providers also
improve by learning from the student \autocite{fan_fedmkt_2025}. The third is formal
privacy guarantees beyond the requirement that provider data never leaves the
provider. The fourth is logit-level and cross-tokenizer distillation, which the
black-box assumption makes unnecessary. Evaluation uses single-turn tasks with
verifiable answers, so that rewards can be computed cheaply and without a learned
judge.
