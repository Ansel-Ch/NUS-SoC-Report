\label{chap:Introduction}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:Motivation}

Much of the knowledge a practitioner would like to train on is no longer available
as data. Hospitals, banks and firms rarely release their datasets, but they
increasingly expose models trained on them, through an interface or as open weights.
Alongside these sit general-purpose and specialised models from many developers. For
anyone training a small language model, the resource at hand is therefore less often
a dataset to download and more often a collection of models to learn from, each with
its own competence. On-policy distillation, in which the student learns from its own
responses as scored by a teacher \autocite{agarwal_gkd_2024,gu_minillm_2024}, is a
natural way to learn from such models.

Deciding what a learner should practise next is the subject of curriculum learning
\autocite{bengio_curriculum_2009}. Automated curricula treat the decision as a bandit
problem in which a task is rewarded by the progress the learner makes on it
\autocite{graves_curriculum_2017,matiisen_tscl_2017}, and the idea has recently been
applied to the fine-tuning of language models, with problem categories as the tasks
\autocite{chen_sec_2025}. Distillation from several models adds a second decision to
the curriculum: not only what to practise, but whom to learn it from.

The natural heuristic is to learn from the strongest available model. The evidence
suggests this is a poor rule for small students: models of 3B parameters or fewer
often fail to benefit from larger teachers and learn more from simpler supervision
\autocite{li_learnability_gap_2025}, and a larger teacher does not necessarily yield
a better student \autocite{guo_lada_2026}. Which provider is most useful thus depends
on what the student currently knows, and that changes as the student learns. This
thesis therefore studies a curriculum over pairs of provider and domain, and asks the
following question.

\begin{mdframed}[backgroundcolor=bg, linewidth=0pt, innertopmargin=8pt,
  innerbottommargin=8pt]
Given several white-box providers and different domains, how should a student decide
whom to ask, about what, and when?
\end{mdframed}

This setting is termed provider-selective distillation. It has four properties.

\begin{description}[itemsep=2pt,topsep=4pt]
  \item[Several providers.] The student can learn from more than one model, and no
    provider is assumed to be best in every domain or the best teacher for this
    student.
  \item[White-box access.] The student can read a provider's next-token probabilities
    on any text, which requires the provider to share the student's tokenizer.
  \item[Several domains.] Prompts come from domains that differ in how much the
    student can currently learn on them.
  \item[State-dependent value.] The benefit of learning from a provider on a domain
    depends on the student's current parameters, which the curriculum's own choices
    change.
\end{description}

The last property separates the problem from familiar tools. Online data mixing treats
sources as fixed datasets and adapts their sampling weights with an adversarial bandit
\autocite{albalak_odm_2023,auer_exp3_2002}. DUET tunes a mixture across complete
training runs using Bayesian optimisation \autocite{chen_duet_2026}. LLM routing
selects a model per query at inference time, and no student is trained
\autocite{ong_routellm_2025}. In provider-selective distillation, by contrast, each
decision changes the student and with it the value of every provider at the next
decision. Learning whom to ask and learning from the answer are coupled.

The project first studied a narrower version of the problem, in which providers
return only text and every query has a price, so that the curriculum must fit a fixed
budget. That study is reported in Chapter~\ref{chap:Evaluation}. Its findings led to
the statement above: under a budget the comparison between providers was decided by
their prices, an answer-only reward made a provider little more than a noisy label,
and learning was confined to one domain and unstable
(Section~\ref{pre:sec:refined}).

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:Problem Statement}

\paragraph{Setup.} Let $\pi_\theta$ denote a student policy with parameters $\theta$,
and let $\mathcal{M} = \{M_1, \dots, M_K\}$ denote $K$ providers. Provider $M_k$ is
white-box: for any prompt $x$ and partial response $y_{<j}$ the student can read its
next-token distribution $p_k(\cdot \mid x, y_{<j})$, over a vocabulary shared with the
student. Prompts are drawn from a pool partitioned into $D$ domains,
$\mathcal{X} = \mathcal{X}_1 \cup \dots \cup \mathcal{X}_D$, and every prompt has an
answer that a program can check. The student is evaluated by a utility
\[
  U(\theta) = \sum_{d=1}^{D} w_d \, \mathrm{Acc}_d(\theta),
\]
its weighted accuracy on held-out sets, one per domain, with weights $w_d \ge 0$
summing to one.

\paragraph{Interaction.} Training proceeds in rounds. At round $t$ the selector
observes a context $s_t = \phi(\theta_{t-1})$ summarising the student's state, such as
its per-domain accuracy on a selection set, and chooses an arm $a_t = (k_t, d_t)$, a
provider and a domain. A batch $S_{k_t,d_t}$ of $b$ prompts is drawn from
$\mathcal{X}_{d_t}$, the student answers them, and it is updated under the supervision
of $M_{k_t}$,
\[
  \theta_t = \operatorname{Update}\!\big(\theta_{t-1}, S_{k_t,d_t}\big).
\]
The sequence of arms $a_1, a_2, \dots$ is the curriculum.

\paragraph{Objective.} A selector is a policy $\pi_{\mathrm{sel}}$ that maps the
history of contexts, arms and rewards to the next arm. There is no query budget. The
scarce resource is the student's own training, so a selector is judged by its learning
curve, the utility $U(\theta_t)$ as a function of the number of prompts $t\,b$ the
student has trained on. A good curriculum makes the curve rise quickly and end high:
for a horizon of $T$ rounds it should attain a high $\mathbb{E}[U(\theta_T)]$, and a
high utility at every earlier point. The selection set that supplies the selector's
feedback is disjoint from the test sets on which results are reported.

\paragraph{Marginal value.} The quantity the selector must implicitly estimate is the
marginal value of arm $(k, d)$ at student state $\theta$,
\[
  g(k, d; \theta) =
  \mathbb{E}\big[\, U\big(\operatorname{Update}(\theta, S_{k,d})\big) - U(\theta) \,\big].
\]
A selector that knew $g$ would play the arm of highest marginal value at every round.
In practice $g$ is unknown, is observed only through noisy evaluations, and changes
with $\theta$, so each observation of an arm's value is partly stale by the time that
arm is next considered. If $g$ did not depend on $\theta$, the problem would reduce to
a stochastic multi-armed bandit. Because the selector's actions change $\theta$,
rewards are neither stationary nor independent of the selector's past choices.

\paragraph{Hypotheses.} The formulation is worth studying only if provider value
actually varies in this way, and the framework is worth building only if it helps.
Four hypotheses are therefore tested.

\begin{description}[itemsep=2pt,topsep=4pt]
  \item[H1 (Non-dominance).] At a fixed student state, the provider of highest
    marginal value differs across domains.
  \item[H2 (State dependence).] Within a fixed domain, the provider of highest
    marginal value changes over the course of training.
  \item[H3 (Adaptivity pays).] With fewer training prompts, an adaptive curriculum
    reaches the utility of the best fixed curriculum.
  \item[H4 (Passing the teachers).] A student trained on several providers ends
    above one trained on the best single provider, and can exceed the providers' own
    accuracy.
\end{description}

H1 alone justifies a fixed assignment of providers to domains. H2 is what makes an
adaptive curriculum necessary, and H3 is what makes it worthwhile. H4 asks whether
several teachers are worth more than one. H1 to H3 were first tested in the budgeted
version of the problem (Chapter~\ref{chap:Evaluation}), where value is measured per
unit of price.

\paragraph{Assumptions.} The following assumptions hold for the refined problem. There
is a single student. Providers are fixed and honest, though they may be weak in some
domains. Every provider shares the student's tokenizer. Training prompts have checkable
answers, so a ground-truth reward is available during training.

\section{Objectives and Scope}
\label{sec:Objectives}

The objectives of this project are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt,topsep=4pt]
  \item Formalise provider-selective distillation as a curriculum over providers and
    domains, as stated above.
  \item Build a reproducible testbed with a small open student, several providers and
    three domains with checkable answers, and measure provider value in it.
  \item Design a training objective that combines a reward from ground truth with
    the selected provider's token probabilities, and that is stable and makes progress
    in every domain.
  \item Design curriculum selectors that learn from the student's progress, and
    compare them with uniform allocation, single providers and fixed orders, by
    learning curves.
  \item Test whether a student trained on several providers can pass the best single
    provider and the providers themselves.
\end{enumerate}

Three directions lie outside the scope of this project. The first is strategic
behaviour by providers, which turns the problem into one of mechanism design
\autocite{sim_cml_2020}. The second is mutual transfer, in which providers also improve
by learning from the student \autocite{fan_fedmkt_2025}. The third is distillation
across tokenizers, which the preliminary study found lossy and fragile
(Section~\ref{pre:sec:motivating}). Evaluation uses single-turn tasks with verifiable
answers, so that rewards can be computed cheaply and without a learned judge.
